# Pedagogy and apps for a Sun–Earth–Moon teaching app

Briefing for the design session. Facts only, no design proposals. Claims carry an inline URL;
anything I could not confirm from a primary source is marked **(unverified)**.

---

## 1. Conceptual progression by age

The most useful framing in the literature: astronomy learning requires moving between two frames
of reference — the Earth-based view (what you see in the sky) and the space-based view (the model
that explains it). Plummer et al. show this shift is the core difficulty, recurring for day/night,
phases, eclipses, tides and seasons (https://juliaplummer.com/papers/PlummerWaskoSlagle2011.pdf).

### (a) Day/night from Earth's rotation

- **Age 5–6:** children readily accept that Sun and Earth are separate spheres; far fewer
  attribute day/night to rotation, and the simultaneous spin+orbit is what confuses them
  (https://doi.org/10.1080/096697600111725). A small quasi-experiment (n=16, age 5) found most
  children could explain day/night by rotation after modelling + video + object imitation
  (https://revistes.urv.cat/index.php/ute/article/view/2959) — small sample, indicative only.
- **Age 7–8:** untutored French and Cameroonian children: a few held heliocentric explanations, and
  in both countries most conceptions were *fragmented* rather than coherent
  (https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11422-018-9897-6).
- **Age ~9 (grade 3):** about half still work from naive mental models; the rest explain the Sun's
  apparent motion scientifically but much less so for the Moon and stars
  (https://juliaplummer.com/papers/PlummerWaskoSlagle2011.pdf). Vosniadou & Brewer (grades 1/3/5)
  found a few consistent "initial", "synthetic" and (rarely) "scientific" models, synthetic ones
  persisting into grade 5 (https://doi.org/10.1016/0364-0213(94)90022-1).
- **Standards expect it by grade 5:** NGSS ESS1.B puts "day and night; daily changes in the length
  and direction of shadows" at end of grade 5
  (https://www.sdcoe.net/ngss/dimensions-of-the-ngss/disciplinary-core-ideas/dci-earth-and-space-sciences/ess1b-earth-and-the-solar-system).
  AAAS's Atlas of Science Literacy places rotation-explains-day/night in grades 3–5 (quoted in the
  Plummer PDF above).
- **Known hard:** the *overall* cause is learned better than sunrise and sunset specifically —
  Sun-motion thinking survives for sunrise/sunset after instruction, visible in children's gestures
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7326024/). Rotation cannot be physically experienced,
  which the authors argue is why gesture/embodiment helps.
- **Risk:** reading a science text can *create* new misconceptions in children whose prior model is
  incompatible — they form new synthetic models rather than replacing the old one
  (https://doi.org/10.1080/09500693.2017.1361557).

### (b) Seasons from axial tilt + orbit

The hardest of the four, and the one with the most durable wrong answer.

- **Standards defer it to grade 8.** MS-ESS1-1 (6–8): "Earth's spin axis is fixed in direction over
  the short-term but tilted relative to its orbit around the sun. The seasons are a result of that
  tilt and are caused by the differential intensity of sunlight"
  (https://www.sdcoe.net/ngss/dimensions-of-the-ngss/disciplinary-core-ideas/dci-earth-and-space-sciences/ess1b-earth-and-the-solar-system).
  The grade-5 expectation 5-ESS1-2 carries the assessment boundary "Assessment does not include
  causes of seasons", quoted verbatim in
  https://science.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/nasa-eyes-for-museums-ngss.pdf.
- **Age does not fix it.** Across grades 3, 5, 7 and 9, use of "distance theory" did **not** decline
  with age; it survives as long as the scientific explanation is not well understood or is
  forgotten (https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2044-8279.1998.tb01308.x).
- **Adults fail too.** *A Private Universe* (1987): graduating Harvard seniors, including physics
  students, confidently explained seasons by orbital eccentricity
  (https://serc.carleton.edu/resources/1672.html). In junior high, about as many students chose
  varying Earth–Sun distance as chose axial tilt (https://doi.org/10.1080/09500690010025085).
- **Why hard:** needs three ideas at once — orbit, fixed tilt, and the *spread* of parallel sunlight
  on a sphere. Reviews find only interventions addressing all three improve understanding
  (https://doi.org/10.1080/09500690903214546; slides at
  https://studylib.net/doc/5438461/lelliott_100315---communicating-astronomy-with-the-public).
- **The counterintuitive fact:** Earth is *closest* to the Sun in early January (147.1 M km) and
  farthest in early July (152.1 M km) (https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/earthfact.html)
  — northern winter is perihelion. A visibly eccentric drawn orbit reinforces the wrong model:
  research on 13–14-year-olds found "Earth's elliptical orbit" among the most influential features
  of textbook images on students' drawings and explanations
  (https://journals.aps.org/prper/abstract/10.1103/PhysRevPhysEducRes.14.010145).

### (c) The year as one orbit

- Evidence is thin and indirect. NGSS places "the orbits of Earth around the sun … cause observable
  patterns … at different times of the day, month, and year" at grade 5, but names seasons as the
  grade-8 item (https://www.nextgenscience.org/disciplinary-core-idea/ess1b-earth-and-solar-system?page=1).
- The known failure mode is **interference between the day cycle and the year cycle**: children
  build hybrids such as "the Earth goes around the Sun once a day" (Baxter's notion 5,
  https://doi.org/10.1080/0950069890110503) — a coherent-looking attempt to explain day/night with
  the orbit they just learned. Plummer et al. list the "days, months, years" timescales as a
  specific reasoning demand (https://juliaplummer.com/papers/PlummerWaskoSlagle2011.pdf).
- Whether children of a given age reliably grasp "one orbit = one year" as a *duration* claim is not
  well established in the sources found — **(unverified)**.

### (d) Solar and lunar eclipses

- **Earliest realistic target: grade 5–6.** Kavanagh, Agan & Sneider conclude third grade is too
  young, that instruction is supported from fifth/sixth grade, and that the NSES expectation of
  mastery by grade 8 is ambitious but is what the standards ask
  (https://doi.org/10.3847/aer2005002).
- **Hard for a specific reason:** the dominant error is conflating phases with eclipses — "lunar
  phases occur when the Moon enters the Earth's shadow" is described as the most prevalent
  misconception, in children and adults (https://doi.org/10.3847/aer2005002;
  https://pubs.aip.org/aapt/pte/article/55/5/314/277597/Addressing-students-misconceptions-about-eclipses).
- A fifth-grade drawing study (n=443) found perceptions of shadow, solar and lunar eclipses "mostly
  wrong", with children unable to draw a shadow even when they know the word — I could not retrieve
  the primary PDF, so treat the figures as **(unverified)**.
- Understanding eclipses requires the Moon's orbital plane to be inclined ~5° to the ecliptic, which
  is why eclipses are not monthly — a geometric idea most curricula skip
  (https://science.nasa.gov/moon/eclipses/).

## 2. Misconceptions and what is shown to work

### Documented misconceptions

| Misconception | Status in the literature |
|---|---|
| **Seasons caused by distance to the Sun** | The most durable misconception in the field; survives schooling and does not fade with age (https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2044-8279.1998.tb01308.x); held alongside the correct tilt answer by ~half of junior-high students (https://doi.org/10.1080/09500690010025085); held by Harvard graduates on camera (https://serc.carleton.edu/resources/1672.html). Related: "tilt oscillation" — the axis swinging back and forth (https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1085430.pdf). |
| **Day/night because the Sun goes behind Earth / hills / clouds** | A documented sequence of six notions, from "Sun goes behind hill" through "clouds cover the Sun", "Moon covers the Sun", "Sun goes around Earth once a day", "Earth goes around Sun once a day", to "Earth spins once a day" (Baxter, ages 9–16: https://doi.org/10.1080/0950069890110503). Naive views decrease with age but persist to 16. |
| **Eclipses happen every month** | Widely reported; caused by ignoring the 5° inclination (https://science.nasa.gov/moon/eclipses/). Also predicted as a *consequence of bad diagrams*: they "may lead students to believe … that solar and lunar eclipses occur every month" (https://pubs.aip.org/aapt/ajp/article/85/7/550/1039667/Learning-about-the-scale-of-the-solar-system-using). |
| **A solar eclipse is visible everywhere on Earth** | Listed as a common initial idea (https://doi.org/10.3847/aer2005002). |
| **Phases caused by Earth's shadow** | Same sources; also what *A Private Universe* found in graduates. |

### Interventions with evidence

- **Constructivist / activity-based beats direct instruction**, especially when prior ideas are
  elicited first; models and own observations beat textbooks, 2-D images and lecture
  (https://doi.org/10.3847/aer2005002).
- **Physical and hands-on models.** Hands-on models produced "scientific" rather than "synthesis"
  models in 7th graders and learning was more durable than control
  (https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/02635143.2017.1401532). Refutational modelling —
  students actively refute their own misconception with apparatus — improved preservice teachers
  significantly (https://doi.org/10.3847/aer2008005). A purpose-built physical model of parallel
  sunlight on a tilted sphere beat controls on seasons and retained better
  (https://doi.org/10.1007/s44217-025-00519-1).
- **Predict–observe–explain with 3-D computer modelling** produced conceptual change on seasons and
  Moon phases (https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/0031-9120/43/6/011).
- **Conflict maps** (discrepant event + explicit resolution) produced change still measurable at 8
  months, while rote recall regressed (https://doi.org/10.1002/tea.20089).
- **Student-centred exploration beats teacher-guided presentation** in a technology-enhanced seasons
  course (F=28.05, p<0.001): students who could test their own hypotheses moved further
  (https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/sce.20242).
- **Viewpoint change.** Interleaving Earth-based sky views with space views benefits schoolchildren
  learning daily celestial motion (https://juliaplummer.com/papers/PlummerWaskoSlagle2011.pdf);
  immersive dome versions of identical seasons and scale lessons outperformed flat-screen versions
  (https://doi.org/10.19030/jaese.v2i1.9276;
  https://pubs.aip.org/aapt/ajp/article/85/7/550/1039667/Learning-about-the-scale-of-the-solar-system-using).
- **Embodiment/gesture.** Children who gesture Earth rotation while explaining show better verbal
  understanding; rotation "may be better conceptualized with the enactment of a corresponding
  gesture" (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7326024/).

### Two documented failure modes of well-meant interventions

- **The shared flashlight-and-ball demo.** With one light and one ball at the front, "not all the
  children in the class saw the Moon ball from the same angle", and the teacher usually moved the
  *flashlight* to change phase — reinforcing the wrong causal agent. The reviewed recommendation is
  that each student gets their own materials (https://doi.org/10.3847/aer2005002).
- **The dual-perspective textbook figure.** Illustrations showing both a space view from above the
  pole and eight Earth-based Moon views are hard for young children *and for adults who don't
  already understand phases*, because they demand two simultaneous viewpoints
  (https://doi.org/10.3847/aer2005002).
- **Scale-up warning.** A three-week hands-on seasons unit (GEMS *The Real Reasons for Seasons*,
  8 activities, grades 6–8) was evaluated with 4,777 seventh graders and rated by the What Works
  Clearinghouse as having **potentially negative effects** on general science achievement
  (https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/InterventionReports/wwc_gemsseasons_012913.pdf). Sound pedagogy
  is not evidence of learning gains.

## 3. Survey of existing apps and interactives

|---|---|---|---|

Does not teach day/night, seasons or eclipse geometry as such; NASA's educator kit maps it to MS-ESS1-1 via a "eclipses in Eyes" viewing exercise (https://science.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/nasa-eyes-for-museums-ngss.pdf) |

| **Universe Sandbox** (https://www.universesandbox.com/education/) | Free physics sandbox; editable properties; N-body; VR | Advertises "a true-to-scale solar system"; VR used explicitly for sense of scale | Physics and collisions. | **Solar System Scope** | Free camera with heliocentric / geocentric / panoramic modes; child-oriented presentation | Not stated in the sources read — **(unverified)** | Widely used in classrooms; one quasi-experiment (n=36, grade 7) with Universe Sandbox reports gains over conventional instruction (https://ejournal.unhasy.ac.id/index.php/ed-humanistics/article/view/11459) |
| **Star Walk Kids** (mobile, AR) | Point the device at the sky; gyroscope maps the real sky; narrated facts | N/A — sky view only | Sky identification and facts; the review notes no assessment or application task (https://www.commonsensemedia.org/app-reviews/star-walk-kids-astronomy-for-children) |
| **Museum kiosks** | Single station, trackball/touch, topic menu | Usually not stated on screen | Maryland Science Center Science-on-a-Sphere kiosk: 70% of natural users viewed only one or two of six topics, but when staff *invited* them, visitors explored significantly more topics and features and parents participated fully (https://www.noaa.gov/sites/default/files/legacy/document/2019/Jun/MSC_SOS_Kiosk_Rep.pdf). NISE Net *Sun, Earth, Universe* (families with children 6–12): mean dwell 5 min 31 s, 89% enjoyment, but content is not Earth–Sun–Moon mechanics (https://nisenet.org/sites/default/files/catalog/uploads/nise_network_seise_project_evaluation_sun_earth_universe_exhibition_summative_evaluation_10-7-20.pdf) |

Gap, stated narrowly: none of the surveyed tools pairs an Earth-based sky view with a space-based model *and* explicit misconception handling for seasons and eclipse frequency — an observation about this set, not a claim that nothing exists.

## 4. Scale representation

### The arithmetic problem

At Voyage's 1:10-billion scale the Sun is a 13.9 cm sphere (a large grapefruit), the Earth 15 m away
and about 1.3 mm across, Pluto 600 m down the street (http://voyagesolarsystem.org/the-experience/).
NASA JPL states the constraint plainly: to show sizes and distances at one scale, "you'll need to
spread your model across at least half a mile"
(https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/edu/resources/project/make-a-scale-solar-system/). For the Sun–Earth–Moon
system (computable, no citation needed): Earth–Sun is ~108 Sun diameters, Earth–Moon ~30 Earth
diameters. Both numbers are the entire content of "why eclipse alignment is special", and both are
invisible in any single-screen diagram.

### What outreach professionals recommend

1. **Do not attempt one scale for everything.** JPL's informal-education guidance says the learning
   objective can legitimately be just "that the scale of the planet distances and the scale of their
   sizes are, by necessity, vastly different for most drawings/models", and that "just getting
   students to acknowledge and understand the inaccuracies of drawings or models can be enough for
   one session" (https://informal.jpl.nasa.gov/museum/content/scale-solar-system).
2. **Choose the scale around the human, not the physics.** Voyage's designers picked 1:10 billion so
   a human body is roughly midway in orders of magnitude between the smallest object depicted
   (a 1,000 km moon, 0.1 mm) and the largest distance (Sun–Pluto, 600 m). Stated rationale: this
   "maximises the visitor's ability to directly observe both the sizes of the worlds and the
   distances between them — at the same time"
   (http://voyagesolarsystem.org/teacher-resources/voyages-scale/). They explicitly rejected
   classroom scale, where the model Sun would be <0.1 mm and the planets invisible.
3. **Separated scale modes are an established, documented pattern.** PhET ships exactly this: a
   "Model" tab not to scale for manipulability, and a "To Scale" tab with a measuring tape, the
   guide explaining that having both "helps to emphasize how big the real solar system is"
   (https://phet.colorado.edu/files/teachers-guide/gravity-and-orbits-html-guide_en.pdf).
4. **Label the distortion on the same screen as the distortion.** Earth Space Lab prints "Distances
   and dimensions are not in scale" inside the scene (https://www.earthspacelab.com/app/seasons/).
   The counter-example is documented: reviews single out not-to-scale diagrams carrying no note, and
   recommend communicators actively "help people to interpret what book diagrams and Internet images
   represent" (https://studylib.net/doc/5438461/lelliott_100315---communicating-astronomy-with-the-public).
   The same review notes correct scale can itself fix a misconception — seeing the true Earth–Moon
   distance argues against "Earth's shadow causes phases".
5. **AAAS Benchmarks makes the same-scale physical model a required experience** for grades 6–8,
   "as distinct from most illustrations, in which distances are underrepresented by a factor of 10
   or more", adding that "models with three dimensions are preferable to pictures and diagrams"
   (https://www.project2061.org/publications/bsl/online/index.php?chapter=4).
6. **Immersive presentation of scale measurably helps; flat screens much less.** The same
   Powers-of-Ten-style scale lecture gave large gains in a dome and modest, less-retained gains on a
   classroom display (N=973)
   (https://pubs.aip.org/aapt/ajp/article/85/7/550/1039667/Learning-about-the-scale-of-the-solar-system-using).
7. **Caution on "true scale everywhere".** A true-to-scale Moon orbit is nearly circular (~10%
   distance variation), so accuracy makes the ellipse *harder* to see: both exaggerated and
   true-scale figures distort something, so the choice must follow the learning objective
   (https://www.manoa.hawaii.edu/exploringourfluidearth/physical/tides/tide-formation-tide-height/practices-science-scaling).

No source recommends a single canonical fix beyond: choose the scale from the learning objective,
keep size-scale and distance-scale separable, and state the distortion on screen.
