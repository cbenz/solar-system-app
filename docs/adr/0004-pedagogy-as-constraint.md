# Pedagogical findings are constraints, not preferences

Four rules that read as arbitrary choices to anyone who has not read the sources behind them, and that a future contributor would otherwise "fix":

1. **Orbits are drawn nearly circular.** An exaggerated ellipse appears only as a hypothesis to be refuted, never as an explanation. A visible eccentricity in textbook images is documented as shaping students' explanations toward the distance theory of seasons, and the Earth is closest to the Sun in early January anyway.
2. **Light is always derived from the Sun's real position.** A shared ball-and-flashlight demonstration at the front of a class is documented as teaching the wrong causal agent: with one lamp for everyone, children see different things, and the demonstrator changes the phase by moving the *lamp* (Kavanagh, Agan & Sneider 2005). Here nothing emits or redirects light except the Sun, and the child changes a phenomenon by moving bodies or time, never by moving a lamp. In the sandbox the Sun itself is draggable, because there its position is the thing under test — and the lighting follows it.
3. **POV and space view are never fused into one figure.** A single drawing that asks the child to hold two viewpoints at once is documented as hard for children *and* for adults who do not already understand the phenomenon. The app keeps them as separate panels with separate frames of reference, which is what makes three POVs side by side legitimate: one frame, three places. The banned object is the dual-perspective diagram, not the coexistence of the two panels.
4. **Every activity runs predict → manipulate → observe → explain.** The prediction comes before the reveal; that ordering is what the retention evidence rests on.
